## 1 Introduction

The elections to the Student Organisation of the University of Ljubljana (ŠOU) and to the student organisations of the individual faculties (ŠOVZ) are a key moment for all students. In them we decide who will represent us for the next two years and how the money we ourselves contribute will be spent. ŠOU manages almost 5 million euros a year, which come from the concession levy on student work – that is, directly from our pockets.

This year's elections, taking place on **14 October 2026**, are an opportunity to change that. The Tenzor list stands for a transparent, democratic and student-friendly ŠOU that truly serves its purpose. Our programme offers concrete solutions to the housing crisis, affordable food, better student work, more accessible reading rooms and public transport, and the democratisation of decision-making. We also strive to abolish the privileges of officials and redirect funds to those who need them most – students.

We invite you to cast your vote in October 2026 and support the Tenzor list. Together we can build a student organisation that is accountable, honest and effective. Your vote counts!

## 2 Why organise

Behind the scenes of the parties, events and perks of student life lie the housing crisis, inaccessible and unhealthy food, rising living costs and, with them, the necessity of exploitative student work. None of these problems are unique to students; they reflect social inequalities. If we want to eat, live and study, we have to organise!

In which official structures can students organise?

| Structure | What they do |
| --- | --- |
| Student Organisation of UL (ŠOU) | Parties, (often private) events, non-transparent spending, fees for student officials, calls for proposals tailored to benefit individuals, upkeep of the unvisited and dysfunctional Campus, and representation of its own financial interests. |
| Student councils | Dealing with bureaucratised procedures for resolving study problems and relations with teaching staff. |
| Local student clubs | Parties, picnics, local concerts, extra subsidies for local bus transport, affordable printing and copying. |

The structure whose primary mission is supposed to be representing the political interests and rights of students in Ljubljana is ŠOU. But do the current form of ŠOU and the coalition that has ruled it for a decade really represent our interests and fight for our rights? The existing structures do not represent our well-being but their own narrow interests, which are often at odds with the interests and needs of students. We can only fight to improve our position together and organised! Entering the existing structures will give us greater oversight of finances and violations, insight into the non-transparent workings of the organisation, and the chance to improve them in line with students' actual needs. We will fight for a better, more stable and kinder tomorrow!

## 3 The Student Organisation of the University of Ljubljana (ŠOU)

The Student Organisation of the University of Ljubljana (ŠOU) is the largest and best-funded student organisation within the “official” structures of student organising, and also the most wasteful. ŠOU's mission is supposed to be representing the interests and rights of the student public at large, yet for decades the organisation has been held hostage by the interests of a narrow circle of student officials and middle-aged bureaucrats who benefit financially from ŠOU directly.

ŠOU's main source of funding is the concession levy on student work: the Student Organisation receives 2.8 % of every hour worked through a student work referral. This means that we students directly fund ŠOU's activities and very existence, yet we get nothing from it in return, because our miserable material position is in its interest!

ŠOU thus operates without any serious mission; its interests are not the interests of students, and its activities are limited to organising sports events, parties and events that serve themselves. Through its (in)action ŠOU preserves the status quo, keeping students in an apolitical position that suits only ŠOU's officials.

We stand for an organisation that will be a true representative of students' interests and rights and that will put students first, not itself!

The reasons for the diametrical opposition between the interests of ŠOU and those of students lie in the organisation's structure. ŠOU is built in a way that prevents it from starting from students' actual position when tackling systemic problems:

| ŠOU's interests | Students' interests |
| --- | --- |
| As much precarious student work as possible, to keep securing its main source of funding. | More time for quality study, less work needed to survive. Public services (canteens, housing, scholarships …) that would reduce the need for student work. |
| More student meal vouchers used, each bringing ŠOU a €0.04 commission. Preserving the current system, which brings it an extra €150,000 a year for its maintenance. | Affordable, healthy food that students can afford every day. |
| An apolitical attitude across the whole student population, which preserves a system that gives ŠOU a lot and students little. | A representative that responds to social and political developments in the country, at the university and among students. |
| Students being uninformed about ŠOU's internal affairs. | We students want to know clearly who represents us, how and where. |

Improving students' material conditions is not in the student organisation's interest, because it would lose its main source of funding. That is why ŠOU's current budget is directed mainly at organising parties, fees for its officials and other projects through which our money flows into private pockets. The organisation's undemocratic and non-transparent nature is evident as well: ŠOU's interests harm the student population, which is why the organisation excludes students at large from its work.

## 4 Improving students' material conditions

The prerequisite for quality study is decent living conditions – a home, food, a place to study, enough time for study, financial stability. That is why we will fight to improve students' material conditions!

| Area | What is the current situation? | What do we need and how do we get it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **FOOD** | A shrinking number of providers; constantly rising subsidies that barely keep up with ever-higher prices. The meal-voucher system allows no long-term solutions, because it is built for the profit of restaurateurs and ŠOU. | A call for proposals and a fund to build public canteens, a higher subsidy, removing ŠOS/ŠOU from the student meal system. |
| **DORMS** | Too few student beds; existing dorms are run down (the newest was built in 2006). | A dorm fund and construction of new dorms, renovation of dorms, use and renovation of existing infrastructure, a maximum rent cap. |
| **SCHOLARSHIPS** | Scholarships too low to cover even the rent in a public student dorm. | Aligning scholarship amounts with the real costs of study and living. Access to financial aid for the underprivileged. |
| **READING ROOMS** | Reading-room seats offer study space for only 10 % of students. | More capacity, longer opening hours for libraries and reading rooms, modernisation. |
| **FOREIGN STUDENTS** | Discrimination against foreign students; the requirement of a bank guarantee to study in Slovenia (for students over 26, with the threat of reintroducing it for everyone). They are not eligible for student dorms or state scholarships. Leto+ is a completely untenable system of teaching Slovenian because of too few places. | Abolishing forms of discrimination, introducing scholarships for foreign students. |
| **PUBLIC TRANSPORT** | For students, public transport to their place of study is often not a reliable option or causes problems – reaching smaller towns requires many transfers, lines are rare and overcrowded. Having a car is more reliable, but it is a big expense for students, who usually share it with family. On top of all that, some faculties are also victims of poor LPP planning, with too few lines for students. | Not just cheaper tickets, but a comprehensive overhaul of integrated public transport so that it serves students' needs; frequent night lines to larger centres, openness to democratic initiatives, cooperation with student clubs to put pressure on official institutions (DUJPP); cooperation with the ŠOVZs and pressure on LPP to provide additional lines. |
| **HIDDEN COSTS OF STUDY** | On top of enrolment fees and contributions for libraries and the student council, students have to pay for laboratory equipment and software, compulsory excursions and field work, and study materials. These burden students of the natural-science faculties, FA, ALUO and PF the most. They can amount to several hundred euros per year. | Hidden costs of study must be fully covered. Everything needed for quality, successful study must be available to students free of charge. Free access to and free reproduction of study materials must be ensured. The university must guarantee truly equal opportunities for all, and ŠOU must apply constant pressure to make that happen, while also establishing a fund to cover the costs of the poorest students. |

Because these preconditions for quality study and life are not guaranteed, many of us students have to rely on exploitative student work to cover rising costs. Student work is a social corrective meant to give poorer students the same starting point as everyone else. But the need for such a corrective exists only because public funds are being cut in favour of profits, which we can observe both in the state and in ŠOU.

We students are paid only an hourly rate with no meal or travel allowance, our hourly rate is often lower than that of regular employees, employer violations (lower pay than agreed, undeclared work, disguised employment) most often go completely unpunished for lack of inspections, and our jobs are insecure, since the employer can dismiss us at any time. Student status is so profitable for employers that more and more students report paying for a fictitious student status because their employer will not hire them properly. And since ŠOU is funded by the levy on student work, we constantly hear student officials praising this form of employment and encouraging its expansion. Student life should be devoted to study, not to making profit for employers and student officials! The precondition for this is, first and foremost, improving students' material conditions – which poses an existential threat to the Student Organisation.

## 5 Democratising ŠOU

This state of affairs can only reproduce itself by excluding students at large from democratic processes. The first necessity, then, is to wrest the management of ŠOU from the hands of alienated student officials and directors who get rich at our expense, and to abolish all the mechanisms that exclude students from decision-making!

### 5.1 Participatory budget

We propose establishing an independent, digital (participatory) fund decided on by the student community, to ensure more democratic management of students' money. The fund would finance activities that ŠOU's current work does not cover, thereby empowering organisations outside ŠOU and grassroots student initiatives the organisation has so far ignored. This also includes financial aid for students in need, for which the current budget sets aside less money in total than for parties (€175,000 versus more than €300,000 for parties). ŠOU's budget should be directed first and foremost at improving students' material conditions. But as long as the budget is in the hands of well-paid officials, this part of it is limited to extorted sweeteners.

### 5.2 ŠOVZs

A ŠOVZ, or student organisation of a higher-education institution, is the local form of ŠOU present at every faculty. It is named after its faculty, e.g. ŠOFSD, ŠOPF, ŠOFMF, ... Joint lists of three (at smaller faculties) or seven (at larger faculties) people stand for these bodies, with one or two of them standing for a seat in the Student Council.

ŠOVZs receive a monthly amount from ŠOU that their members manage. With this money we can actually address students' needs. Projects such as free photocopying, a textbook fund, student canteens and so on are examples of sensible use of student money – returning it to students' pockets.

ŠOVZs, however, are financially very limited and cannot be the main means of improving students' material conditions. Until the matter is resolved systemically, though, they remain the best way to actually help students. The ŠOVZs at individual faculties know their own students' needs best, so we demand that they be given more autonomy and more funding!

### 5.3 Referendum

All major student questions are decided by the Student Council (ŠZ), whose legitimacy is fragile because of low turnout. ŠOU is not willing to listen to the opinions of students at large, as is evident from the budget itself. Important decisions for the student population should be made in student referendums and other forms of direct participation. The restriction of students' democratic participation is also evident in the conditions for calling and passing a student referendum, which can be called only by a two-thirds majority in the Student Council or with several hundred signatures, and requires at least 3% turnout at every faculty, which is practically impossible. We demand a lower referendum quorum so that students can actually decide on matters concerning their money!

### 5.4 Digitalising elections

ŠOU's democratic deficit is most visible in the elections to the Student Council and the ŠOVZs. Turnout is dismal every time (between 1 % and 5 %), so the results do not reflect the will of students at large. This naturally suits the ruling coalition, which actively keeps students ignorant of ŠOU's structures and of any possibility of democratic involvement. Part of this is its opposition to digitalising the elections, which would make voting considerably easier and more accessible. We demand more information about the elections and ŠOU's structures, and a digital voting process!

### 5.5 Democratising elections

Standing in ŠOU elections is very difficult for anyone outside the coalition lists. Among other things, the coalition appoints the electoral commission, which rules on rule violations with bias and can even impose an unconstitutional fine on a given list, which it then cannot collect. The electoral commission should be an independent body, unconnected to any political option within ŠOU; fines should be abolished and replaced by more consistent sanctioning of violations.

### 5.6 Student publishing house

We will fight to re-establish a student publishing house, like the one that once existed. The new publisher will not serve commercial activities and the interests of capital, but the wishes and needs of students in literature and theory, and will make it easier for students to publish contributions, articles, and bachelor's and master's theses.

### 5.7 Radio Študent

Radio Študent (RŠ) faces extremely low funding from its founder, ŠOU. It is one of the few public media institutions in Slovenia and the only student medium of its kind. Because of its critical reporting on ŠOU, RŠ has trembled at every budget vote and been blackmailed by the ruling Blue-Connected (Modro-Povezana) coalition. In 2021 the Student Council even wanted to cut RŠ's funding entirely. These circumstances push Radio Študent's journalists into worse material conditions, while the radio itself must compete for various state and other grants and is thus left to the (un)mercy of changing governments. We demand higher and stable funding for RŠ so that it can truly operate as an independent and autonomous student medium, and its journalists must be properly paid for their work!

### 5.8 Student campus

The Student Campus functions as a space for the student elite and for parties. This has been true since the land was bought, for which ŠOU paid 1.2 million euros to a former student official who had bought the same land only three months earlier for 350,000 euros. The pattern has repeated ever since. Although it is a public space that we all have the right to enter, in practice it serves as a private club for the ŠOU elite, who profit further at students' expense by charging twice for parties (a party is funded first with student money through ŠOU and then again through entrance fees). The Campus must therefore serve students' interests (reading-room seats, social evenings, free rental options, space for students) or be sold, since the average student gets nothing from it!

## 6 Finances and what to do with ŠOU

For decades ŠOU has relied on non-transparent financial practices. Even the adoption of the budget is non-transparent, as budget explanations are always sent out only a few days before sessions. Financial reports are not always public, and when they are, they appear in an inadequate form; a running record of spending does not exist at all. Most worrying of all is where the money goes: 60 % of all funds are spent on ŠOU's bureaucratic apparatus alone – session fees for council members are €55 per session, the presidency spends €180,000 every financial year, and the director alone receives a monthly salary of €5,000 (plus allowances the director pays out personally). The ŠOU director has held on to this salary for more than a decade, even though the director's term is supposed to be limited to two years. Nor are external service providers publicly known, or the reasons for choosing them, which allows former student officials, now small business owners, to draw from this financial pool long after leaving office.

Instead of addressing students' ever-worsening material conditions with anything more than empty petitions, our money is directed at reproducing an organisation that primarily serves the enrichment of student officials and a select circle of business owners. This reflects fundamental problems in the very way ŠOU gets its money – from student work. Structurally, the organisation's main purpose cannot be working for students' good. Its money simply cannot flow anywhere other than into events that exist for their own sake and, of course, into the absurdly high salary of the director, council members' session fees and deals with former officials. Our goal therefore must not be merely reforming ŠOU, but abolishing its main source of funding. Most of students' problems are not rooted in higher education but in the wider socio-economic system. That is why we cannot stand up to them within such a narrow field of action alone. ŠOU as an institution formally advocates students' interests, but since these do not differ from the interests of most of the rest of the population, we must be aware that ŠOU is only a limited means of creating the conditions we want. Student work referrals could be issued by the Ministry of Labour instead of private student employment agencies, and the state should likewise take students' problems seriously, which would at the same time remove the need for exploitative work: building public housing and canteens, a reliable public transport network, covering the hidden costs of study, etc. Social events could equally be handled by existing structures outside ŠOU.

Our goal in the Student Council and the ŠOVZs is therefore, first and foremost, the fight against structural problems found both in ŠOU and in wider society. In the Student Council we constantly call out and oppose the Blue-Connected coalition's enrichment at students' expense, and in the ŠOVZs we prioritise directing money to the areas that matter most to students.

## 7 Review of the opposition's and coalition's work in the previous term

### 7.1 Work in the Student Council

In the 2024/2026 term, thanks to an unimaginable election victory, the number of opposition members in the Glas caucus rose from 5 to 13. The opposition was suddenly harder to ignore and silence, which is also reflected in the halved number of Student Council sessions compared with the 2022/2024 term: from 16 sessions down to 8.

One of the main issues of the term was the question of recording Student Council sessions. Since the 12th regular session of the previous term (7 December 2023), recording Student Council sessions had been prevented by the opposition of the Blue-Connected coalition. Radio Študent tried to record the 14th regular session, but the coalition walked out and thereby obstructed it. From then on the Student Council was guarded by security staff who checked bags for recording devices.

The opposition to recording continued into the following term, as shown in the table below, which sets out how coalition and opposition members voted. At this year's constitutive session, too, we were met by security staff, and the media were prevented from video-recording the session. The coalition now allows only audio recording, which still prevents fully transparent operation and lets members hide. The procedural motion for video recording of sessions, which the Glas caucus submitted before every session, was rejected every time.

| Session | Motion | Glas caucus | Blue-Connected | Adopted or rejected |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Constitutive session, 1st and 2nd regular sessions | Procedural motion for video recording of the session | All FOR | Against and abstained | Rejected |
| 2nd regular session | ŠOU appoints Andrej Klasinc as ŠOU director for a 4th term. | All AGAINST | All FOR | Adopted |
| 2nd regular session | (Controversial) founding of the Zavod 365 institute | All AGAINST | All FOR | Adopted |
| 3rd, 4th and 5th regular sessions | Procedural motion for video recording of the session. | All FOR | All for, except Student Council president Oskar Škulj. | Recording adopted for the first time in the term (valid for that session only). |
| 3rd regular session | Blue (Modri) amendment to the Glas proposal for systemic regulation of session recording. | All FOR | FOR and abstained. | Rejected |
| 3rd regular session | (Unlawful) Connected (Povezani) amendment to the Glas proposal on session recording. | Walk out in protest | - | Connected withdraw their proposal |

As can be seen, the trend changed at the 3rd regular session. Before the session the opposition held a press conference on ŠOU's non-transparent practices, putting the coalition under strong media pressure. Coalition members suddenly began voting for temporary recording of sessions, which they had previously opposed so fiercely. They even began proposing their own amendments to the rules of procedure, although it took the additional pressure of the opposition's protest walkout from the 3rd regular session before they finally proposed a systemic and lawful regulation of Student Council session recording at the 5th regular session. Before that, of course, they rejected every opposition proposal. In the end, the very people who had initially prevented session recording were the ones who signed off on it.

As the table shows, the Glas caucus was also active in other areas during the term. At the constitutive session it immediately demanded that ŠOU clearly condemn the ongoing genocide in Gaza, which of course fell on deaf ears. It successfully ensured that the rules of procedure state that ŠOUL is a legal person governed by public law. It tried to reduce session fees for council members and other student officials, for whom almost €500,000 is set aside in the current budget. The proposal was of course rejected on the argument that student officials deserve every bit of the student money currently flowing into their pockets, if not more. Glas members voted against the blind founding of yet another expensive institute with no clear plan. They also tried to increase the Student Council's transparency and democracy by allowing members to yield the floor to people without a seat. This proposal, too, was rejected in the Student Council because of the Blue-Connected coalition's opposition, justified by the argument that it would create chaos in the Student Council.

### 7.2 Work in the ŠOVZs

We also delivered on our programme at the ŠOVZ level. There we everywhere gave up session fees and moved them into the project budget; at several faculties (FF and FSD) we provided a free student printer available to everyone, provided free menstrual products, held several free student canteens, set up a free textbook fund (FF), co-funded the supply of study materials (ALUO), twice ran a participatory budget project at the faculty level (FDV), and secured an autonomous student space (FSD). We co-funded faculty student societies and field trips.

At FMF, among other things, we founded the Tenzor library, held free canteens, organised a mafia picnic, climbing, ice skating, skiing, a field trip to ITER, Red Week and 8 March, set up a billiards and foosball table at Physics, brought back the tea corners at Mathematics and Physics, and collected study notes in one place.

On top of all this, we also provided a colourful range of extracurricular activities in the form of free picnics, excursions and, of course, parties. We secured free entry to concerts by Rotor Motor, Matter, a student music night (Delta Riff and Milena), Jacoby and many others. Together, the opposition lists organised more than 60 fun events. At many of them we provided free drinks through vouchers. In all our work we made sure above all that money went into improving students' material conditions, and with fun events we took care to organise free parties open to everyone whenever possible. Unfortunately, ŠOU and the coalition lists did not follow this practice, even with far greater funds, because it rules out any personal profit and actually directs money to students.

## 8 Conclusion

We have seen that once we set aside the interests of student officials and the narrow circle of business owners into whose pockets our money flows, there is no longer any good reason for such an expensive and ineffective structure as ŠOU to exist at all. After all, its own structure prevents it from tackling students' truly pressing problems in any meaningful way. Organising is therefore also necessary outside the existing structures, in the form of gatherings, networks, political events, canteens, reading circles, assemblies, etc. The goal must be to build a broader student community, connected with the wider working class, that can escalate pressure to achieve our demands in every area that affects us. Only when broadly connected and well organised can we produce enough pressure for the exploitative conditions built into the socio-economic system itself to truly change for the better! Individual effort is not enough to change existing practices, because we can turn the existing balance of power in our favour only through collective action and a clearly defined goal!
